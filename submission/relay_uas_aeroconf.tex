\begin{abstract}
An airborne relay can restore a radio path across an obstruction, but the aircraft must carry and power the payload long enough to provide useful service. This paper examines which placement and dwell targets warrant further development for a notional multirotor relay. The architecture assigns mission traffic to the payload and aircraft command, electrical power, and mechanical support to the carrier. That allocation defines two screens: a link check on both relay hops and a carrier check that closes the battery, mass, and hover-power loop. At 10 km separation and 120 m relay height, the reference 30-minute case closes at 4.77 kg with a 0.50 m rotor, while the 45-minute case exceeds both size limits. Moving the relay toward the ground endpoint raises the weaker-hop margin by about 1 dB without changing modeled hover demand, which makes it a provisional candidate for site assessment. Published hover data constrain one power coefficient. Fitting it to each test point alone moves the rotor-size boundary from 33 to 49 minutes, and pairing the least favorable point with a 15 percent hover-power allowance places that boundary near 28 minutes. The 30-minute target is therefore a candidate that installed hover-power measurements must confirm. Site-specific link measurements and a payload-fault check address the other risks to that decision. The contribution is a traceable path from each failed screen to the design decision and interface test it calls for. Return traffic, full-mission energy, and installed-system performance remain outside the assessment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Consider an operator at a ground station who is tracking a remote aircraft on a map but cannot reach it by radio because an obstruction sits on the direct path. The operator does not need a stronger radio as much as a better vantage point. A relay aircraft provides one. Placing a radio above the obstruction can provide two clear paths, one to each endpoint. Research on unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) has examined this opportunity through aerial networking and mobile relaying \cite{zengmag}, \cite{tutorial}, \cite{zengrelay}. The aircraft must sustain the useful geometry while carrying and powering the radio. This makes the communications requirement an aircraft resource requirement as well.

The hard part is keeping the relay there. Suppose the two endpoints are 10 km apart, the remote aircraft flies at 100 m, and an 80 m screen stands between them. A midpoint relay at 120 m clears the screen in the declared geometry. Whether it can remain there for 30, 45, or 60 minutes depends on a different calculation. More battery adds mass, greater mass requires more hover power, and that power requires additional battery. A consistent mass balance can lie within a small-aircraft envelope, lie outside it, or have no finite solution under the assumed scaling relations.

The design question is what to carry forward: a different relay position, a longer dwell, or a different carrier. The architecture assigns mission traffic to the payload and aircraft command, electrical power, and mechanical support to the carrier. These responsibilities establish what each analysis must check. The study first asks whether both radio hops pass, then whether a consistent aircraft can sustain the requested dwell within the size limits. It uses the results to recommend a candidate for further assessment and identify what could overturn that recommendation. Section~\ref{sec:arch} defines the service and system boundary. Section~\ref{sec:methods} presents the models and evidence, Section~\ref{sec:results} compares the resulting choices, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusions} identify the next decisions and tests. The appendices retain the derivation and calibration details.

\subsection{Related Work and Contribution}

Communication and aircraft sizing research already supplies the pieces used here. Zeng, Zhang, and Lim optimized relay transmit power together with relay trajectory \cite{zengrelay}. Zeng, Xu, and Zhang combined rotary-wing propulsion energy with communication energy under throughput constraints \cite{zengenergy}. Yan et al. treated battery weight as a design variable that trades stored energy against the power needed to lift it \cite{yan}. Rodrigues et al. placed relay positioning inside a propulsion-aware energy model \cite{rodrigues}. On the aircraft side, Russell, Theodore, and Sekula built conceptual sizing models for small quadcopters from test data \cite{russell2018}. They reported that their sizing did not close for hover missions beyond a certain length. Bershadsky, Haviland, and Johnson matched motors, propellers, and batteries to mission requirements \cite{bershadsky}. Table~\ref{tab:prior} places these alongside the present study.

This paper makes three primary contributions. First, it states an explicit aircraft--payload allocation of four flows and uses that allocation to decide which analysis or test each flow requires. Second, it pairs a two-hop link screen with a carrier screen that closes battery, mass, and hover power, and it compares placement and dwell choices against both. Third, it retains both failure flags for every case and maps each failed screen to a design decision and to the interface or installed test that could overturn the recommendation. In this paper, system architecture means this bounded allocation and its assessment. The study does not compare every possible relay architecture, and it offers no new link-budget equation, sizing law, or optimization algorithm. Selected practices from the NASA Systems Engineering Handbook \cite{nasaSEH} organize the trace from service need to criteria, allocation, and candidate design. This trace matters because it shows which evidence would change the decision.

The research question is whether the declared 10 km, 30-minute outbound service target merits further development, and whether placement or carrier changes should come first. The 20 km separation and 45-minute dwell objectives provide more demanding comparison points. This is an analyst-defined case study without stakeholder-approved requirements or built hardware. Its quantitative scope is one-way radio-path screening during hover; delivered throughput, return traffic, and full-mission performance are not established.

\section{System and Assessment Boundary}\label{sec:arch}

The relay aircraft carries a communications payload and holds an assigned position. In Figure~\ref{fig:arch}(a), traffic travels from ground endpoint G through the payload to remote aircraft U. The payload is a black box for this assessment: its accommodation demands are 0.20 kg of mass and 14 W of direct current (DC) power, and the link model uses a fixed radio frequency (RF) output. The carrier supplies the airframe, propulsion, battery and power distribution, avionics, command radio, and mount. Operators, ground equipment, the remote aircraft, and spectrum authorities are external to the relay system.

Only the time on station is assessed quantitatively. A full mission would run from preparation through launch, transit, establishing position, relaying, monitoring, and recovery. This paper calculates the relay interval alone, and only in the direction from G through the relay R to U. The battery reserve holds energy back from that interval, but it is not a transit or recovery calculation.

\subsection{Aircraft-Payload Boundary}

Four flows organize the system responsibilities, but only electrical power and mechanical support physically cross the carrier--payload interface. Mission traffic passes through the payload; aircraft command stays on the carrier path. Table~\ref{tab:alloc} connects each flow to its failure consequence and the evidence needed next. The calculations address the mission link and the mass and power consequences; command continuity and payload retention require separate evidence. The unresolved interface attributes are identified with each flow below.

\emph{Mission traffic.} Traffic passes from G through the relay payload to U, as Figure~\ref{fig:arch}(a) shows, so the payload owns this flow. The carrier only has to place the payload at the required position and keep it there. The link check establishes that the declared geometry and RF assumptions give two clear hops with positive margin at some sampled points. However, the RF assumptions do not select a radio protocol or waveform, and they do not show that the payload is compatible with the ground radio or the remote aircraft. Data rate, endpoint compatibility, and a delivery criterion remain open. This flow would need an installed two-hop communication test before any service claim.

\emph{Aircraft command.} The operator commands the carrier and receives its health data over a path separate from the relayed traffic. The intent is to retain aircraft control if the payload fails. Sharing a battery and airframe means that logical separation alone is not fault isolation. A payload overcurrent, for example, could disturb the carrier supply unless the power distribution prevents it. A protected payload branch is therefore a candidate design response, not an implemented feature. Its assessment would monitor carrier voltage, command continuity, and fault indication during a safely bounded payload-load fault. Protection thresholds, allowable interruptions, and the carrier's response must be specified before that test can establish compliance.

\emph{Electrical power.} The carrier battery supplies propulsion, avionics, and a regulated payload branch, so the carrier owns the power flow up to the payload connector. The carrier analysis assumes a continuous 14 W payload load behind a regulator with 0.92 efficiency, and it shows how that load and the 25 W auxiliary allowance affect dwell and gross mass. It does not specify the rail voltage, the peak current, the connector, protection, or thermal behavior. This 14 W value is a study assumption for sizing and is not an electrical interface specification. Since no payload radio has been selected, those values remain open, and the rail would need a load test across its specified voltage and current envelope.

\emph{Mechanical support.} The carrier mount retains the payload and transfers its load into the airframe. The model includes the 0.20 kg payload and a mount allowance of 0.20 kg plus 18 percent of payload mass; it does not assess strength or vibration. Detailed design must specify the mount geometry, load envelope, retention criterion, and inspection access. Repository requirement REQ-012 addresses fit, mass, and volume. Retention needs an additional, explicit requirement and supporting inspection and load-test evidence before flight.

This allocation lets the designer change placement without changing the assumed payload installation. Changing the radio is a different decision: similar mass and power do not establish interchangeability. Mounting, voltage, thermal behavior, and electromagnetic compatibility must also match. Table~\ref{tab:alloc} identifies evidence still needed; it is not evidence of a verified installation.

\subsection{Study Measures and Candidate Criteria}

The study represents a ground operator seeking an outbound path to a remote UAS across an obstruction. It does not assume that this path alone restores operational command and telemetry. Link margin, gross mass, and rotor size provide the technical measures and constraints used to screen those targets. Following the distinction in the NASA handbook \cite{nasaSEH}, gross mass and installed hover power are also candidates for technical performance measures (TPMs) once a specific design is tracked through development.

\section{Method}\label{sec:methods}

Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} shows the assessment sequence: define the service, allocate responsibilities, declare the inputs, and screen the link and carrier separately. This structure draws on the NASA handbook's system design processes \cite{nasaSEH} without claiming a completed lifecycle. The baseline grid holds placement at the midpoint and varies separation, height, and dwell. A supplementary analytical comparison moves the relay toward the ground endpoint while retaining the same radio, payload, height, and carrier assumptions. It uses the same path-loss relation and explicitly checks screen clearance. That comparison is separate from the saved 90-case midpoint grid and does not change its counts.

For a fixed payload, the two screens are separable: dwell does not enter the link calculation, and separation and altitude do not change the constant-density hover model. Their intersection is a joint feasibility screen, not a coupled aircraft--radio optimization. The carrier model itself retains the battery-mass-power feedback. The decision rule is to carry forward a candidate that meets the declared target, examine which sensitivities change that result, and specify evidence for the remaining uncertainties. The independent checker addresses implementation consistency; published hover data address only part of the physical model.

\subsection{Declared Inputs}

The study samples declared geometries and durations rather than observed operations. Heights are measured from a common ground datum, with the ground endpoint at 0 m and the remote aircraft at 100 m. The relay sits at the horizontal midpoint at 60, 120, or 240 m. Endpoint separation takes six values from 5 to 60 km and dwell takes five values from 10 to 60 minutes. Table~\ref{tab:inputs} lists these inputs. The baseline scenario places an 80 m opaque screen at 40 percent of the separation. Four variants change the screen's height or position, one variant adds 12 dB of loss, and one comparator removes the screen entirely. These are stylized visibility cases, not terrain.

The payload values come from a commercial mesh radio module with a published mass of 102 g, 5 W receive consumption, 14 W peak consumption, and 1.6 W maximum transmit power \cite{doodle}. The model adds 98 g for unselected integration hardware and, as a sizing assumption, applies the 14 W peak value continuously. The stated maximum RF output and DC consumption have not been verified as a simultaneous operating point. The receiver threshold of $-90$ dBm is one common assumption applied to every receiver, and the 5 MHz bandwidth is recorded as context that never enters the margin calculation.

Each scenario holds 90 cases, formed from six separations, five dwell values, and three relay altitudes. The primary payload is the 0.20 kg, 14 W case. Seven scenarios and three payload cases give 1,890 saved records, which are repetitions of the same grid rather than independent missions, so every count in this paper uses the 90-case denominator.

\subsection{Link Check}

The link check asks two questions about each path: is it clear, and is the received signal strong enough? A path is clear when the straight line between its endpoints passes above the screen. By similar triangles, the relay height at which the first hop begins to clear depends on the screen height and its position along the path.

For the baseline 80 m screen at 40 percent of the separation and a midpoint relay, this threshold is 100 m. This means a relay at or below 100 m leaves the first hop blocked. Heights are compared strictly, so a path that just grazes the screen counts as blocked. In every screened scenario the screen sits on the first hop, so the second hop does not cross it. The screen is an opaque plane, and the model includes no diffraction, Fresnel-zone clearance, or earth curvature.

For a clear path, the check computes free-space loss from distance and frequency with the standard transmission relation \cite{itu525}, \cite{friis}.

The link margin is the received level above the receiver threshold,

\begin{equation}
M=P_t+G_t+G_r-L-L_0-L_x-S,
\end{equation}

where L is the free-space loss. Here Pt is the transmit level in dBm, Gt and Gr are antenna gains in dBi, and S is the $-90$ dBm threshold. L0 is a 6 dB allowance for other losses, and Lx is the scenario loss. The ground endpoint transmits at 30 dBm and the relay transmits at 32.04 dBm, which is the 1.6 W maximum expressed in dBm. A relay path passes only when both hops are clear and both margins are at least 0 dB. The check covers signal level only, so it says nothing about interference, scheduling, or delivered data rate.

\subsection{Carrier Sizing}

The aircraft side of the assessment asks whether an aircraft of consistent mass can hover with the payload for the requested time. Rotor disk area is set by holding disk loading constant at 60 N/m\textsuperscript{2}, so area grows in proportion to mass. Momentum theory then gives ideal hover power, and dividing by the rotor figure of merit and the drive efficiency converts that to electrical power \cite{leishman}, \cite{ndarc}. The result is proportional to gross mass \emph{m},

\begin{equation}
P=k\,m,\qquad k=\frac{g\,k_{env}}{FM\,\eta}\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}},
\end{equation}

where FM is the rotor figure of merit, $\eta$ the drive efficiency, DL the disk loading, $\rho$ the air density, and kenv a multiplier for operating conditions. The calibration identifies only the combined term FM $\eta$/kenv, so the reference relation reproduces laboratory hover power with no margin for operating conditions. At the calibrated values in Table~\ref{tab:inputs}, k is 108.4 W/kg. This means the model describes a family of aircraft that are resized as mass grows, not one airframe carrying a bigger battery. A separate 15 percent hover-power margin would move the carrier boundaries from 42.1 and 52.4 minutes to 35.7 and 44.5 minutes.

The battery must satisfy two separate demands, and the larger one governs. The energy demand covers hover power plus a fixed 40.2 W for avionics and the payload over the full dwell, using only the usable fraction of the pack. The power demand covers the peak current that a 1.75 thrust ratio implies, with a further 15 percent allowance. Dividing each demand by the pack's specific energy or specific power gives a battery mass, and Appendix A states both relations.

Holding back 20 percent after a 90 percent depth of discharge leaves a usable fraction of 0.72. This reserve is part of the mission policy rather than the calibration.

The reference screen includes a 20 percent battery reserve, a 1.75 thrust ratio, a 15 percent peak-power allowance, and 6 dB of other link loss. These are separate allowances, not one interchangeable safety margin. In particular, the hover calibration absorbs the declared operating-condition multiplier and supplies no additional operating-power margin. A separate 15 percent hover-power stress case tests that omission. The allowances must ultimately be justified against the chosen equipment, mission, and acceptance criteria.

Adding the remaining masses closes the loop. Propulsion mass scales with peak power and rotor area, and structure carries a base mass plus a fraction of everything it supports. The payload, avionics, power electronics, and mount contribute a fixed 1.116 kg, and Appendix A defines each allowance. Since every term is either fixed or proportional to gross mass, each battery branch reduces to a fixed mass plus a growth factor times the trial mass.

The growth factor is the fraction of any added kilogram that the aircraft must add again as battery, propulsion, and structure. The energy branch grows with dwell, while the power branch does not. At each trial mass the model takes the larger battery requirement.

\subsection{When the Aircraft Closes}

A consistent sizing result is a fixed point, where the mass implied by the resource calculation equals the trial gross mass. Each branch gives a candidate equal to its fixed mass divided by one minus its growth factor, and Appendix A states the tests a candidate must pass to count as a solution.

The denominator explains why a longer dwell can have a disproportionate mass penalty. As the governing slope approaches one, a small increase in demand produces a large increase in closed mass. Once the energy slope reaches one, its positive intercept prevents a finite positive fixed point. This is a property of the assumed model, not a universal endurance limit for multirotors.

At the reference inputs, power governs the 10- and 20-minute examples, while energy governs the 30- and 45-minute examples. For the architecture, this means the carrier's limiting resource shifts from delivered power to stored energy as the requested dwell grows. The implementation checks this analytical test with a separate iterative solution, described in Appendix A.

\subsection{Calibration and Check}

The hover calibration estimates one aggregate coefficient from five hover test points reported in the 2018 NASA technical memorandum \cite{nasatm}. For each point, ideal induced power uses the measured supported thrust and the sum of that test article's nominal rotor swept areas. Measured electrical power is voltage multiplied by the sum of motor currents. An unweighted least-squares fit through the origin relates measured electrical power to ideal induced power. The fit identifies the combined effect of rotor, drive, and operating conditions, not an independently measured rotor figure of merit. Its residuals range from -18.6 to +14.6 percent. The Phantom 3 point is the largest disagreement: the fitted relation gives 140.1 W against a published measurement of 172.1 W. That point is kept in the calibration set, and the fit does not remove the disagreement.

Because the fit passes through the origin, each point's influence scales with the square of its ideal power; the DAx8 carries 79 percent of that weight. The two highest-leverage observations were also collected inside the wind-tunnel test section, as the 2016 AHS Forum paper describes \cite{russell2016}. To expose this dependence, the analysis also refits the coefficient to each test point alone. These single-point refits measure sensitivity to the selected calibration point and give no statistical bound on endurance; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the boundaries they produce. Appendix B examines other estimators and documents the DAx8 eight-disk area definition against the source photographs.

The structural intercept is inferred separately from the NASA conceptual-design mass breakdown \cite{russell2018}. That breakdown describes a sized baseline design rather than a weighed vehicle. The calculation reproduces its airframe allowance after subtracting the present model's structural growth and disk-area terms, and several carried masses in that subtraction are model allowances. The resulting 0.163 kg is therefore conditional on those choices. It is not a directly measured empty-airframe mass or a separately validated structural law. The reference specific energy, 192 Wh/kg, comes from the published TB30 pack energy divided by its mass \cite{djim30}. That reference does not establish the assumed 900 W/kg continuous power capability for a selected relay battery. It is also the lowest pack value among the check aircraft. The Matrice 4 pack stores 99.5 Wh in 401 g, or 248 Wh/kg \cite{djim4}, and Section~\ref{sec:results} uses that value as a battery sensitivity case.

The calculation uses the published maximum-hover discharge condition without the relay mission's depth-of-discharge and reserve reductions. Differences range from -6.7 to +3.2 percent (Table~\ref{tab:calib} and Figure~\ref{fig:calib}). The auxiliary allowance matters for the two smaller aircraft. Only the Matrice 30, whose hover power is large relative to the allowance, stays within 10 percent across a 0 to 40 W sweep (Appendix B). The two smaller aircraft are therefore consistent with the fitted relation rather than an independent test of it. The Matrice 350 figure is excluded because it describes forward flight. These comparisons provide a limited consistency check on hover power and energy accounting. They do not test the structural mass model, flight control, or a physical relay aircraft, and three published values do not establish a general prediction-error bound.

\subsection{Size Limits and Decision Order}

A closed aircraft is screened against two size limits that keep the result in the small relay class. Gross mass must not exceed 15 kg, and the equivalent rotor diameter must not exceed 0.75 m. The diameter comes from the total disk area shared equally across four rotors.

At the reference disk loading the rotor limit binds first, since a 0.75 m rotor corresponds to a gross mass of 10.81 kg. A separate span check is twice the rotor diameter, so it repeats the rotor limit rather than adding a new one. These limits screen results, and nothing in the analysis prevents a larger aircraft from existing.

Each case then receives one label from the first check that fails. If the direct path passes the assumed service screen, the case is \textsc{direct}: relay benefit is not required by this criterion. Otherwise, if either relay hop is blocked or short of margin, the case is \textsc{link fail}. Otherwise, if the aircraft has no solution, the case is \textsc{no solution}, and if it has a solution outside the size limits, the case is \textsc{too large}. What remains is a \textsc{pass}, meaning a blocked or insufficient direct path, two passing outbound relay hops, and a finite carrier within the exploratory limits. Because connectivity is checked first, a \textsc{link fail} label can hide an aircraft failure in the same case, so the case records keep both flags.

\subsection{Verification and Validation Plan}

The evidence separates implementation checks from physical comparisons and planned installation tests. The documented equations are evaluated by scripted analysis code that generates every case record. That code carries 35 automated tests, and a separate checker rebuilds each link from coordinates, solves the mass balance by independent bisection, and compares its results with the saved records. This shows that the implementation follows the equations, although it cannot show that the equations predict a particular relay. Five published hover observations calibrate one aggregate coefficient. Commercial-aircraft hover times provide a limited external consistency check, with the auxiliary-load sensitivity described above. Neither the structural model nor the installed radio paths have corresponding validation evidence.

Verification asks whether a product meets its specified requirements; validation asks whether it serves its intended use \cite{nasaSEH}. Early analysis and review can support those activities without a flight-ready aircraft. Here, the missing evidence is more specific: the service criteria have not been agreed with an operator, and no integrated relay has been assessed against an accepted requirement set. The modeled passes therefore support design selection, not a claim of installed-system compliance. Before hardware acceptance, each relevant criterion must name the configuration, conditions, observable result, and acceptable threshold.

Table~\ref{tab:verify} records the existing evidence and planned checks using analysis, inspection, demonstration, and test. It is a planning matrix, not a set of completed procedures. For the candidate carried forward, the key checks are the two installed radio hops, hover power with the payload fitted, and command continuity under a bounded payload fault. Their acceptance thresholds remain to be specified. An operational trial would then assess the agreed service with an operator at a representative site, including the mission phases omitted from this model.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Where the Envelope Ends}

The baseline supports further assessment of the 10 km, 30-minute target, but not the 45-minute objective with the reference carrier assumptions. Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the midpoint grid, and the link and carrier boundaries explain its labels. At 120 m, the midpoint relay reaches a calculated 25.0 km separation before the ground-to-relay hop runs out of margin. Adding 12 dB of loss reduces that boundary to 6.3 km, below even the 10 km target. More battery cannot repair this failure while the radio assumptions stay fixed. The designer must reconsider placement, radio performance, or the requested separation. The carrier boundaries below answer a different question: how long a radio path that passes can be supported.


The reference carrier reaches the 0.75 m rotor limit at 42.1 minutes. At that point gross mass is 10.81 kg, so the rotor limit is tighter than the 15 kg mass ceiling. Finite closure persists until the energy-branch slope reaches one at 52.4 minutes. This means the interval between these boundaries holds aircraft that close mathematically but fail the physical screen. Figure~\ref{fig:closure} shows this transition without treating the divergent branch as a buildable aircraft.

The existing favorable and adverse parameter sets illustrate dependence on carrier assumptions. The favorable set gives a 126.9-minute physical boundary and a 157.0-minute energy-slope crossing. The adverse set has no physical pass even at zero dwell; its 6.4-minute energy-slope crossing is not an achievable service duration. These sets change several assumptions simultaneously. They are deterministic corners, not a confidence interval, and the reference is not a statistical midpoint.

The decision should not use 42.1 minutes as available endurance. Refitting the hover coefficient to each test point alone moves the rotor boundary from 33.0 to 49.2 minutes and the energy-slope crossing from 41.2 to 61.3 minutes. A separate 15 percent hover-power allowance moves the reference boundaries to 35.7 and 44.5 minutes. Since both stresses act through the power coefficient k in (2), they compound when applied together. Pairing the least favorable refit, the Phantom 3 point, with the 15 percent allowance places the rotor boundary near 28 minutes; at 30 minutes that carrier closes only at about 16 kg with a 0.92 m rotor. The other four refits paired with the allowance keep the rotor boundary between about 35 and 42 minutes. This means the 30-minute target passes each stress applied alone but fails the least favorable pairing. Therefore, the study carries 30 minutes forward as a candidate that installed hover-power measurement must confirm, and it defers any 45-minute commitment.
Substituting 248 Wh/kg while holding the other battery assumptions fixed moves the reference boundaries to 54.4 and 67.7 minutes, but that sensitivity does not establish a compatible pack with the required power capability. A longer dwell would therefore need a justified change to the carrier itself, such as a qualified higher-energy pack or lower installed hover power, because adding battery to the same design raises the mass that the battery must lift.

\subsection{One Geometry, Three Outcomes}

The worked case fixes the geometry and varies only dwell, which isolates the aircraft behavior. At 10 km separation the screen stands 4 km from the ground endpoint. The direct line passes it at 40 m, below the 80 m top, so the direct path is blocked. With the relay at 120 m the first hop passes the screen at 96 m and is clear, as Figure~\ref{fig:arch}(b) shows. The two hop margins are 7.97 and 10.02 dB, and they do not change with dwell. This is what makes the case useful: every difference that follows belongs to the aircraft. The saved record also lists a 5.96 dB direct margin, which is the margin the direct path would have if the screen were not there.

Table~\ref{tab:worked} shows what changes instead. At 10 and 20 minutes the battery is sized by the power it has to deliver rather than the energy it has to store. This means both cases close at the same 3.58 kg, and the installed 231 Wh is more than either dwell needs. At 30 minutes energy takes over, the aircraft closes at 4.77 kg with a 0.50 m rotor, and the battery reaches 42 percent of gross mass. At 45 minutes the aircraft still closes, but only at 15.15 kg with a 0.89 m rotor, which fails both size limits. At 60 minutes each added kilogram of battery calls for more than another kilogram to lift it, so there is no aircraft to report at all. The last two rows are the two failure stages from Section~\ref{sec:methods} seen in one geometry.

The short-dwell result also needs a scale check. Adding the assumed 0.20 kg payload to the published masses gives 3.97 kg for the Matrice 30 and 1.42 kg for the Matrice 4. Under the fitted power relation and stated energy policy, both yield about 22 minutes, compared with the 3.58 kg, 20-minute reference case. These are calculations, not demonstrations that either aircraft accepts the payload installation. The reference family's 1.116 kg fixed allowance approaches the 1.22 kg Matrice 4 mass in Table~\ref{tab:calib}, and its short-dwell pack is power-sized. That bookkeeping helps explain the difference; it does not prove that a custom carrier is preferable. Selecting an actual airframe remains the next step for the 30-minute candidate.

\subsection{The Sampled Grid}

The 90-case grid shows where those two limits fall across the sampled points. Of the 90 baseline cases, 18 pass, 6 are too large, 6 have no solution, and 60 fail the link check. Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the pattern. Every case at 60 m fails because the relay sits below the 100 m clearance threshold. At 120 and 240 m the 5, 10, and 20 km cases clear the screen and hold margin. Within those cases, dwells from 10 to 30 minutes pass, the 45-minute cases are too large, and the 60-minute cases have no solution. At 30 km and beyond the relay can see both endpoints but the first hop falls short of margin, which matches the 25.0 km limit above.

Of the 90 cases, 30 pass the link check and 54 pass the carrier check; 18 pass both. Table~\ref{tab:joint} retains the joint failures hidden by the ordered labels. Because the screens depend on separate grid coordinates, their intersection follows from the model structure and reveals no new physical coupling. Its value is diagnostic. Of the 60 link failures, 24 also fail the carrier screen, so improving only the radio path would not recover them. This is why the assessment keeps both flags for every case. Link failures dominate this selected grid, which need not represent an operating population. At 30 minutes and 120 m, both the 10 km threshold and 20 km objective pass nominally.

\subsection{Scenario Sensitivity}

Adding 12 dB of loss leaves 6 passes, 2 too large, 2 with no solution, and 80 link failures. Only the 5 km cases retain both hop margins; at 10 km the first hop falls to $-4.03$ dB. Carrier results remain unchanged. This stress case reverses the nominal 10 km service decision, so site-specific link evidence matters before committing to that target. It does not establish a universal test priority: the 12 dB excursion is analyst-selected, and the other sensitivity cases vary different quantities over different ranges.

Moving the screen changes the first-hop clearance threshold and therefore changes which altitude layers work. Figure~\ref{fig:thresh} shows the five thresholds. Lowering the screen to 60 m or moving it to 0.45 of the span drops the threshold to 75.0 and 88.9 m. This leaves the sampled results unchanged, since 60 m is still blocked and 120 m is still clear. Raising the screen to 100 m or moving it to 0.30 of the span raises the threshold to 125.0 and 133.3 m. This blocks the 120 m layer and halves the passes to 9. While the two lower thresholds produce no change at all, that is a statement about the sampled altitudes rather than evidence of insensitivity, since no altitude between 75 and 120 m was tested. For siting, the relay height has to be chosen against the actual obstruction, because a screen that raises the threshold above 120 m removes that altitude layer from service.

\subsection{Placement Choice for the 30-Minute Candidate}

The midpoint is a useful baseline, but it need not be the preferred site. Under the declared asymmetric transmit powers, shifting the relay toward G balances the two hop margins at approximately 0.442 of the endpoint separation. This follows by finding each hop's allowable slant distance at zero margin, converting it to horizontal distance at the stated heights, and placing the relay in proportion to those distances. The rounded position describes a candidate and makes no claim of optimality under real terrain and antenna constraints.

Table~\ref{tab:placement} compares the two placements at 10 km separation, 120 m height, and 30 minutes. The midpoint leaves about 10.0 dB on the second hop but only 7.97 dB on the first, while the offset leaves about 9.0 dB on each, and both first hops clear the 80 m screen. The calculated separation boundary rises from 25.0 km to about 28 km. Since the comparison uses the same free-space and opaque-screen assumptions as the baseline, it does not establish installed coverage.

Both positions retain the reference 4.77 kg carrier and 0.50 m rotor because placement does not enter the hover model. The roughly 1 dB gain on the weaker hop is useful because it costs nothing in the carrier screen, but it is modest beside the 6 dB other-loss allowance and cannot absorb the 12 dB stress loss. The offset is therefore carried forward provisionally for site assessment, with the midpoint retained as the reproducibility baseline. A real siting decision must also account for access, terrain, antenna patterns, command coverage, and the energy needed to reach and leave the position.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What to Carry Forward}

The evidence supports carrying the 10 km, 30-minute target forward, with a relay near 0.442 of the span at 120 m as the candidate for site assessment and the midpoint case as the comparison baseline. This is a recommendation to investigate a configuration, and it does not extend to procuring or flying one. While the offset improves the weaker hop under the declared geometry, the carrier result is the less certain half of the decision. The 30-minute target survives each hover stress applied alone, but the least favorable calibration point combined with the 15 percent power allowance moves the rotor boundary below it. Installed hover-power measurement therefore bears directly on whether the dwell target holds. Neither result removes the need to select compatible hardware or confirm the intended service with an operator.

The present carrier evidence does not support a 45-minute commitment. Its nominal 15.15 kg result already exceeds both size limits, and the calibration sensitivity changes whether that dwell is supportable. At 60 minutes the reference relations have no finite mass solution. These are different failures. The first asks for a change in size, resources, or demand, while the second requires a change in the scaling assumptions or demand before resizing can help. Relaxing the mass ceiling therefore cannot repair mathematical nonclosure.

The useful systems result is a diagnosis tied to an action. Each failed screen points back to one of the four flows. A weak radio path is a mission-traffic problem and calls for a siting or radio decision. An excessive carrier is a power and mechanical-support problem and calls for a dwell or resource decision. A payload fault that can disturb aircraft command sits where the power and command flows meet, and it calls for an interface decision. This is the practical value of the allocation, although it does not rank all possible architectures. In particular, a radio upgrade would need its RF benefit, mass, DC load, antenna installation, and compatibility assessed together. A gain in one screen is not automatically a gain for the complete relay.

The next evidence should address whichever uncertainty can reverse the intended decision. Agreeing the service comes first, including return traffic, delivery criteria, dwell, and reserve policy, since those criteria define what a compatible payload--carrier candidate must satisfy. Where site loss is poorly known, installed measurements of both hops at the midpoint and offset placements would test the link half of the recommendation. Where dwell drives selection, repeated installed hover-power measurements that record mass, battery state, and operating conditions would test the carrier half, and a later energy budget would add climb, transit, and recovery. Where command independence is essential, the bounded payload-fault assessment described in Section~\ref{sec:arch} would test the power and command interface. Each test needs acceptance criteria defined before it runs. These activities can proceed in parallel, and the largest change in an analyst-selected sensitivity sweep does not by itself justify testing one item first.

\subsection{Limitations}

The carrier model describes a resized family at constant disk loading, not an existing aircraft with interchangeable batteries. Its aggregate hover fit does not resolve scale effects, rotor interference, or environmental variation. The DAx8 dominates the fit, and the two highest-leverage points were measured in the tunnel with unquantified recirculation. The single-point range and the combined pairings with the 15 percent allowance are sensitivity results, and they do not bound all model error. Other pairings of a calibration stress with an individual battery or structural assumption were not examined. The commercial hover comparisons use nominal battery energy and an assumed auxiliary load, so small discrepancies may include compensating assumptions.

The structural relation is anchored at one conceptual mass breakdown. Its extrapolation becomes especially consequential near the closure singularity. Neither that anchor nor the power fit establishes packaging, strength, vibration response, thermal compatibility, or a realizable combination of battery energy and power.

The link calculation tests visibility and received level. It omits interference, fading, diffraction, Fresnel clearance, scheduling, duplex operation, and throughput. These simplifications do not all err in the same direction. An opaque screen treats a grazing path as blocked, whereas knife-edge diffraction would impose a finite loss. However, omitting Fresnel clearance works in the other direction just above the geometric threshold. In the 10 km case, keeping 60 percent of the first Fresnel zone clear raises the 100 m threshold to about 108 m, which the 120 m layer still clears.

The offset-placement comparison is limited to the same ideal screen and fixed antenna gains as the baseline. A more favorable path in this geometry need not be a better installed site: terrain, antenna patterns, interference, command coverage, and mission energy may change the choice. Statistical air-to-ground blockage models address different questions \cite{alhourani}; none is fitted or validated here. Neither the placement result nor the count of grid passes supplies a probability of service availability.

Only stationary outbound dwell is calculated. Transit, climb, recovery energy, return traffic, carrier-command performance, including the carrier's response to a payload fault, and station keeping remain outside the quantitative assessment. A follow-on assessment would need matched power measurements, installed-payload characterization, a complete mission energy budget, and communications measurements for the intended service.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

For the declared 10 km outbound service, the study carries a 30-minute dwell target forward for detailed assessment and identifies a relay near 0.442 of the span at 120 m as a candidate for site evaluation. The reference carrier closes at 4.77 kg with a 0.50 m rotor, and the offset improves the weaker hop by about 1 dB without changing modeled hover demand. The 45-minute reference case exceeds both size limits. However, the 30-minute target does not survive every stress examined, since pairing the least favorable calibration point with a 15 percent hover-power allowance places the rotor boundary near 28 minutes. The nominal 42.1-minute boundary is therefore a model result and should not be read as available endurance.

The contribution is the connection, made through the four-flow allocation, between a failed screen and the next engineering decision. Placement can improve a radio path but cannot cure excessive carrier mass, and additional battery cannot recover a blocked or inadequate link. Before committing to hardware, the service criteria and payload--carrier interfaces must be specified. Site-specific link measurements, installed hover-power measurements, and a bounded payload-fault assessment address different ways the recommendation could fail. Full-mission energy and operational validation remain subsequent work. The result is a candidate worth testing, with a stated reason for each test. It is not a flight-ready relay.

\section{Complete Carrier Mass Balance}
The following equations specify the component bookkeeping used in the results. Mass is in kilograms, power in watts, energy in watt-hours, area in square metres, and dwell $t$ in hours. Write $q=g/DL$, so total rotor disk area is $A=qm$, and let $k$ denote electrical propulsion power per unit mass from (2). Define
\begin{equation}
P_0=P_{\mathrm{aux}}+P_L/\eta_r,\qquad u=d_b(1-r_b).
\end{equation}
The reference values are $P_0=40.2174$ W and $u=0.72$. With thrust ratio $\tau$ and battery power margin $\mu_b$, the battery branches are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_E&=\alpha_E m+\beta_E,& \alpha_E&=kt/(ue),\\
\beta_E&=P_0t/(ue),&\alpha_P&=\mu_b\tau^{3/2}k/p,\\
m_P&=\alpha_Pm+\beta_P,&\beta_P&=\mu_bP_0/p.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Battery mass is $m_b=\max(m_E,m_P)$. Installed nominal energy is $em_b$; on the power branch it can exceed the nominal energy required for dwell. The model applies thrust scaling to propulsion alone.

With the component coefficients in Table~\ref{tab:components}, fixed carried mass and propulsion-group mass are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{mount}}&=m_{m0}+f_m m_L,\\
F&=m_L+m_{\mathrm{av}}+m_{\mathrm{pe}}+m_{\mathrm{mount}},\\
m_{\mathrm{prop}}&=K_pm,\quad K_p=\tau^{3/2}k/p_p+\sigma_rq.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The structural allowance includes all carried mass:
\begin{equation}
m_s=m_{s0}+f_s(F+m_b+m_{\mathrm{prop}})+\sigma_sA.
\end{equation}
Substitution in $m_{\mathrm{new}}=F+m_b+m_{\mathrm{prop}}+m_s$ gives, for branch $j\in\{E,P\}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a_j&=(1+f_s)(\alpha_j+K_p)+\sigma_sq,\\
b_j&=(1+f_s)(F+\beta_j)+m_{s0}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Evaluate $m_j^*=b_j/(1-a_j)$ only for $a_j<1$ and positive mass. The energy candidate is valid when $m_E(m_E^*)\geq m_P(m_E^*)$; the power candidate requires the reverse inequality at $m_P^*$. With no valid candidate, physical mass, area, power, and energy are undefined. A branch tie gives the same fixed point.

\section{Hover Calibration Sensitivity}

The single-point refits in Section~\ref{sec:methods} describe sensitivity to the calibration observations; they do not bound all hover-model uncertainty. The Phantom 3 and Endurance points, whose disk loadings of 67 and 63 N/m\textsuperscript{2} are nearest the reference value, set both ends of that range. Other defensible estimators (mean ratio, geometric mean, quadrotors only) give 41.4 to 44.6 minutes for the rotor limit and 51.5 to 55.6 minutes for the energy-slope crossing. This means the choice of estimator moves the boundaries less than the choice of test point.

The DAx8 area uses eight 11 in rotor disks. NASA lists eight rotors of that diameter, and its test-article photographs show eight laterally separated rotor positions rather than four coaxial pairs \cite[Table 1 and Figs. 5 and 12]{russell2016}. Their summed geometric area is approximately 0.490 m$^2$. The reference calibration therefore retains the eight-disk convention. A four-disk substitution does not represent the documented arrangement and is not used to support a longer dwell. This resolves the geometry question, not the limits of the calibration: rotor interference remains embedded in the aggregate coefficient, and the DAx8 still dominates the fit. The single-point and alternative-estimator checks remain the relevant tests of calibration sensitivity.

The hover-time comparison in Section~\ref{sec:methods} is also sensitive to the assumed auxiliary load. With a 0 W allowance, the Matrice 30, Matrice 4, and Mavic 3M differences become $-1.5$, +17.2, and +30.0 percent. With 10 W they become $-3.7$, +8.2, and +17.8 percent, and with 40 W they become $-9.6$, $-11.9$, and $-8.2$ percent. Only the Matrice 30 remains within 10 percent across this sweep; its calculated discrepancy still depends on the allowance.
